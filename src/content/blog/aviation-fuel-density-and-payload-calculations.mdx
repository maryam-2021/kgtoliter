## Why Aviation Measures Fuel by Mass, Not Volume

In commercial airline operations and military aviation, aircraft fuel is pumped into wing tanks from airport hydrant trucks in **litres or gallons**, but it is entered into the Flight Management System (FMS), weight-and-balance manifests, and dispatch release forms strictly in **kilograms (kg) or pounds (lbs)**.

The rationale for this universal protocol is rooted in basic aerodynamics and engine thermodynamics:

1. **Aerodynamic Lift and Structural Limits:** An aircraft wing does not generate lift based on the space occupied by fuel; it supports total gravitational weight. Maximum Takeoff Weight (MTOW), Maximum Zero Fuel Weight (MZFW), and Maximum Landing Weight (MLW) are structural mass limitations certified in kilograms.
2. **Combustion Energy Output:** Jet engines burn fuel based on mass stoichiometry. The energy content of aviation kerosene is approximately **43.15 MJ per kilogram**, regardless of whether thermal expansion has increased or decreased its volume.
3. **The Infamous Gimli Glider Incident:** In 1983, an Air Canada Boeing 767 ran out of fuel midway through its flight because ground crew and pilots confused litres, kilograms, and pounds when converting fuel density, leading to a deadstick emergency landing.

## Temperature Variation and Density Sampling

Because jet fuel is stored in above-ground airport tanks or underground hydrant networks, its temperature can range from **-20^°C in winter Arctic operations** to **+45^°C on desert runways**.

The thermal expansion coefficient of Jet A-1 is roughly **β = 0.00095 per ^°C**.

### Runway Temperature Impact on Fuel Density
- At -15^°C, Jet A-1 density can climb to **0.825 kg/L**.
- At +35^°C, the same fuel batch drops to **0.785 kg/L**.

Prior to refueling, an authorized refueler conducts a hydrometer check at the bowser:
1. Draw a sample of jet fuel into a clear hydrometer jar.
2. Check for visual contaminants and water separation (Clear & Bright test).
3. Float an ASTM-calibrated aviation hydrometer and record observed density and fuel temperature.
4. Provide the certified density reading directly to the flight crew or dispatcher on the fuel receipt.

## Flight Dispatch Calculations: Payload vs. Tank Capacity

### Scenario: Ultra-Long-Haul Flight Fuel Planning
Consider a Boeing 777-300ER preparing for a trans-pacific flight requiring **120,000 kg of mission fuel**.

#### Case A: Cold Weather Departure (Fuel Density = 0.820 kg/L)
`V = (120,000 kg) / (0.820 kg/L) = 146,341 Litres`

#### Case B: Hot Weather Departure (Fuel Density = 0.785 kg/L)
`V = (120,000 kg) / (0.785 kg/L) = 152,866 Litres`

In hot weather, the aircraft requires **6,525 extra litres of fuel volume** to carry the exact same energy and mass! 
On aircraft operating near maximum fuel tank volume capacity, low fuel density can cause **tank volumetric limitation**, forcing dispatchers to reduce passenger baggage or cargo payload to accommodate the necessary fuel volume.

## Critical Rules for Aviation Fuel Handlers

1. **Never Assume 0.800 kg/L Standard:** While 0.800 kg/L is often used as a rough rule of thumb, certified flight plans require actual bowser hydrometer readings. A 2% error on a 100-tonne fuel uplift represents 2,000 kg of unaccounted mass.
2. **Verify Fuel Cross-Checks on the Flight Deck:** Pilots must cross-check the fuel mass recorded by the aircraft's onboard Fuel Quantity Indicating System (FQIS)—which utilizes capacitive probes that adjust for fuel dielectric constant and density—against the calculated volume from ground fuel delivery tickets.
